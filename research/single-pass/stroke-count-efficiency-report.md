# Stroke Count, Efficiency and Effort: Why Fewer Strokes Can Make Swimming Harder

*A technical review for an experienced intermediate freestyle swimmer (170 cm)*

## Summary

A widely held principle holds that faster swimming comes from more efficient swimming: fewer strokes, longer glide, better streamline. In practice, the swimmer in this review found the opposite. Swimming at 17 strokes per 25m feels light and fluid, while a technically refined 11–13 strokes per 25m with strong glide drives heart rate and breathing sharply upward at any pace.

This report explains why. The central finding is that stroke count is a proxy for efficiency, not a measure of it. True efficiency is the metabolic cost of covering a given distance at a given speed. Reducing stroke count below a swimmer's natural optimum raises that cost through higher force per stroke, uneven speed, sustained isometric tension, and compromised breathing. The report then examines how propulsion actually works, clarifies the relationship between stroke rate and stroke count, applies these principles to a 170 cm swimmer, sets out a method for locating each swimmer's personal optimum, and provides a practical testing and training program.

## Chapter 1: The Efficiency Paradox

### 1.1 The observation

Two versions of the same swimmer produce opposite results. At 17 strokes per 25m, with a quicker rhythm and less emphasis on glide, swimming feels easy and sustainable. At 11–13 strokes per 25m, with long glide and maximum use of every stroke, easy swimming becomes nearly impossible regardless of pace, and heart rate climbs steeply.

### 1.2 The underlying error

The paradox arises from confusing two kinds of efficiency. Hydrodynamic refinement (a longer, more streamlined, lower-count stroke) is not the same as physiological economy (less energy spent per meter). The first is visible and easy to count; the second is what determines how hard swimming feels. When stroke count becomes the goal in itself, it can be improved at the expense of the thing it was meant to indicate.

## Chapter 2: Why Fewer Strokes Can Cost More Energy

Five mechanisms combine to make a very low stroke count physiologically expensive.

### 2.1 Higher force per stroke and muscle fiber recruitment

Swimming speed equals stroke rate multiplied by distance per stroke. Reducing the count from 17 to 11 removes about a third of the strokes, so at a similar pace each remaining stroke must deliver roughly 50% more propulsion, while also compensating for the non-propulsive glide.

Low-force work relies mainly on slow-twitch, oxidative muscle fibers, which are economical and fatigue-resistant. As force per contraction rises, the body recruits more fast-twitch fibers, which consume more energy, produce more lactate and tire quickly. Heart rate and breathing respond to this recruitment pattern.

Cycling offers a direct parallel. Pushing a large gear at low cadence covers ground with few pedal strokes, yet trained cyclists choose a high cadence because high-torque work fatigues the legs and raises perceived effort. The 17-stroke swim corresponds to spinning a small gear; the 11-stroke swim corresponds to grinding a large one.

### 2.2 Uneven speed and the cost of reacceleration

Drag in water rises with the square of velocity, and the power needed to overcome it rises with the cube. For a given average speed, therefore, fluctuating speed costs more than steady speed. A long glide lets the swimmer decelerate, after which each stroke must reaccelerate both the body and the water moving with it (known as added mass). The fast phases cost disproportionately more than the slow phases save.

This resembles stop-and-go city driving compared with steady highway cruising. Research on intracyclic velocity variation generally supports this: larger speed fluctuations within the stroke cycle are associated with higher energy cost.

### 2.3 Isometric holding

A glide-based stroke requires holding the lead arm extended, maintaining a rigid streamline, and bracing the core and hips during non-propulsive phases. Sustained isometric contraction raises pressure inside the muscles and partially restricts their blood flow, which triggers a reflex increase in heart rate and blood pressure. The effect is similar to a wall sit or plank, where heart rate rises far more than the visible work would suggest.

### 2.4 Breathing and carbon dioxide

A slow, glide-heavy rhythm lengthens the time between breaths and makes the breathing window harder to time. Carbon dioxide accumulates, and carbon dioxide, rather than low oxygen, is the main driver of air hunger. Many swimmers also hold their breath during the glide instead of exhaling steadily, which compounds the problem. This is often the largest contributor to how hard the stroke feels.

### 2.5 The muscle force–velocity relationship

Muscle converts chemical energy into work most efficiently at moderate contraction speeds, roughly one third of maximum shortening velocity. A slow, heavily loaded pull can fall below this range, consuming more energy for the work produced.

### 2.6 The optimum stroke count

Each swimmer has a U-shaped relationship between stroke count and energy cost at any given speed. Too many strokes wastes energy through slip and unnecessary movement; too few wastes it through high force demands, speed fluctuation and breathing restriction. For most swimmers of average height, 11 strokes per 25m is well below this optimum; it is lower than many elite distance swimmers use at race pace.

## Chapter 3: The Limits of Adaptation

### 3.1 What practice can improve

Training at a low stroke count will make it easier over time. Part of the current difficulty stems from novelty: an unfamiliar pattern is performed with excess tension and conscious control. Breathing adapts well with practice, particularly continuous exhalation and timing within a slower rhythm. Strength and fiber adaptations allow slow-twitch fibers to handle higher forces, reducing reliance on fast-twitch fibers.

### 3.2 What practice cannot change

The underlying physics remains. At a given pace, fewer strokes still require more force per stroke, and drag still penalizes uneven speed. Practice improves the execution of a glide-based stroke but does not remove its cost.

### 3.3 The role of speed

At a genuinely slow pace, where force per stroke is modest, a count of 11–12 can become comfortable, which is why it works well as a drill and for technical recovery swimming. As pace increases, force demands rise steeply, and the count that feels light will naturally drift upward. Elite swimmers show the same pattern: their counts rise with speed, because adding rate becomes cheaper than adding force. A very low count is therefore unlikely ever to be the most economical choice at training or racing speeds.

## Chapter 4: The Mechanics of Propulsion

### 4.1 Force is not the same as propulsion

Pushing hard against the water and moving efficiently through it seem as though they should be identical, but they are not, because water moves when pushed. Forward thrust equals the mass of water moved multiplied by the change in its velocity. The energy spent, however, equals half that mass multiplied by the velocity squared.

The same thrust can be produced in two ways: moving a large mass of water slightly, or moving a small mass of water a great deal. Because of the squared term, the second approach costs far more energy, which ends up in the wake as turbulent, fast-moving water. A forceful push tends toward this second approach: the hand slips, the swimmer feels high resistance, and much of the effort goes into stirring the water rather than moving the body. Skilled swimmers describe the alternative as "holding water" or anchoring the hand and moving the body past it.

### 4.2 The paddle and the propeller

A paddle pushes straight back against water it has already set in motion. As the stroke continues, the water ahead of the blade is moving backward, so the blade must move ever faster to keep pushing, and slip increases. A propeller sweeps sideways into still water, generating force much as a wing generates lift, and never needs to chase water it has already moved.

A high-rate stroke at maximum effort behaves more like a propeller: each stroke meets fresh water with brief contact. A slow, forceful stroke behaves more like a paddle, with long contact against increasingly moving water.

A historical note is relevant. In the 1970s, coach James Counsilman popularized a lift-based theory of propulsion built on S-shaped sculling pulls. Subsequent research and fluid modeling showed that freestyle propulsion is mainly drag-based, with lift in a supporting role, and modern coaching favors a relatively straight pull with a high elbow. The principle behind the propeller analogy nevertheless holds: effective propulsion keeps the hand working against water that has not yet been set in motion.

### 4.3 Cargo ships and racing boats

Marine engineering makes this trade-off explicit. Cargo ships use large, slow-turning propellers that accelerate great volumes of water gently: highly efficient, but limited in speed. Racing boats use small, fast-spinning propellers that accept more slip in exchange for raw thrust. Distance swimming corresponds to the cargo ship, with a long grip, moderate rate and minimal slip. Sprinting corresponds to the racing boat, where rate rises, grip per stroke falls somewhat, and the extra energy cost is accepted for speed.

### 4.4 Muscle power

Power equals force multiplied by speed. Muscles produce peak power neither at maximum force nor at maximum speed but in between. A slow, maximally forceful stroke sits at the low-speed end of this curve, feeling extremely hard while delivering less power than expected. A faster, moderately forceful stroke often produces more total power, which explains why a high rate feels productive at maximum effort.

### 4.5 The skill to develop

The objective is neither more force nor fewer strokes, but greater hand speed without loss of grip. An effective stroke begins relatively gently at the catch, anchors, and accelerates toward the finish, so the hand keeps pace with the water it is moving instead of pushing against water already in motion.

## Chapter 5: Stroke Rate and Stroke Count

### 5.1 Resolving the apparent contradiction

Raising stroke rate without raising stroke count appears paradoxical but is simply the definition of swimming faster. Stroke count is measured per distance; stroke rate is measured per time. A swimmer taking 14 strokes per 25m at 1.2 seconds per stroke swims for about 17 seconds. Holding 14 strokes at 1.0 second per stroke reduces this to about 14 seconds. Distance per stroke is unchanged, so speed rises in direct proportion to rate.

### 5.2 Why it is difficult

At higher speeds, drag increases with the square of velocity. To keep each stroke covering the same distance, each must produce more thrust, with the hand moving faster relative to the body without slipping. Grip tends to deteriorate as hand speed rises, so count usually creeps upward. Nearly all swimmers, elite ones included, add some strokes as they accelerate. Holding count perfectly is the ideal; keeping the increase small is the realistic goal.

### 5.3 A measure of quality

The ability to move from easy to threshold pace while adding only a stroke or so per length indicates both good grip at higher hand speeds and sufficient power to drive each stroke harder. A large jump in count, for instance from 14 to 19, indicates that speed is being bought largely through slip. The point at which count rises sharply can be called the slip threshold, and moving it to higher rates over time is a clear marker of genuine improvement.

## Chapter 6: Application to a 170 cm Swimmer

### 6.1 Interpreting the numbers

Arm span largely determines how far each stroke can carry a swimmer. At 170 cm, 17 strokes per 25m is a normal, healthy easy count. A count of 13 is reachable only with a substantial glide. What appears to be "good form at 13" is in fact good form combined with a costly glide, and the two should be separated.

### 6.2 Separating form from glide

The technical gains made through low-count work are real: improved body line, better head position, an earlier catch and a firmer grip on the water. These should be retained, while the pause out front is removed. With continuous propulsion and no glide, the count should settle naturally, most likely around 15–16 strokes per 25m at an easy, fluid rhythm. That count, reached without gliding, represents the swimmer's true efficient stroke.

### 6.3 The role of low-count swimming

A count of 13 remains useful as a drill: a few lengths to reinforce length and body position, followed by a return to natural rhythm while carrying that feel forward.

## Chapter 7: Locating the Personal Optimum

No validated universal formula exists for a swimmer's optimal stroke count. A reliable estimate can nevertheless be built in three stages: a calculation that defines the rough range, field tests that establish the upper and lower boundaries, and a cost-measurement set that locates the bottom of the U-shaped curve. The calculation indicates where to look; the testing reveals where the swimmer actually is.

### 7.1 Stage one: estimating the range

Stroke count per length can be approximated as:

**Count ≈ (pool length − push-off distance) ÷ distance per stroke**

In a 25m pool with a typical push-off, roughly 20m is swum. For adult swimmers with sound technique, distance per single arm stroke tends to fall, very roughly, between 0.65 and 0.8 times body height at easy pace. This ratio is a coaching rule of thumb for orientation rather than a research-validated constant.

For a swimmer of 170 cm, this gives a distance per stroke of about 1.1–1.35m and a predicted range of roughly 15–18 strokes per 25m. This agrees with the swimmer's own experience: 17 strokes feels comfortable, 15–16 is a plausible optimum with improved technique, and 13 lies outside the range.

### 7.2 Stage two: defining the boundaries

**Minimum count (the glide floor).** Swim easy lengths with continuous arm motion, in which one arm begins its pull before the other finishes and there is no pause out front. Lengthen the stroke as far as possible without the arms ever stopping. The lowest count achievable this way is the floor. Any count below it depends on glide and falls into the costly zone described in Chapter 2.

**Maximum count (the slip ceiling).** Swim easy lengths with progressively quicker, shorter strokes. The ceiling is reached when the stroke becomes choppy: the hand exits before completing the push, or the water is felt to slip and effort rises without any gain in speed. This typically lies three to five strokes above the natural count.

The optimum lies between these boundaries, usually toward the lower middle of the range.

### 7.3 Stage three: finding the bottom of the curve

The central exercise holds pace constant across a range of stroke counts and measures the physiological cost of each.

**Setup.** Choose an easy-to-moderate pace, for example 25m in 22 seconds. Because pace and count are both fixed, stroke rate follows automatically:

**Tempo Trainer interval ≈ (time per 25m − about 2 s for push-off) ÷ count**

At 22 seconds per length, 16 strokes requires (22 − 2) ÷ 16 = 1.25 seconds per stroke, and 14 strokes requires about 1.43 seconds.

**The set.** Swim 50m repeats at each count from floor to ceiling, for example 14, 15, 16, 17 and 18, with 45–60 seconds of rest. Randomize the order rather than working up or down in sequence, so that fatigue does not bias the results. Immediately after each repeat, record heart rate and perceived effort on a 1–10 scale. A chest strap is considerably more reliable in water than a wrist monitor; alternatively, count the carotid pulse for 10 seconds and multiply by six. Repeat the test on a second day and average the results.

**Interpretation.** Plot heart rate against stroke count. The result is usually a shallow U. The lowest point is the optimum at that pace, and the counts within about 3 beats per minute of it form the practical focus range. The bottom of the curve is often flat across two or three counts, which gives the swimmer latitude to vary the stroke without penalty.

For a mathematical optimum, fit a parabola to the data points, which a spreadsheet can do with a second-order trendline. For a fitted curve HR = a·count² + b·count + c, the optimum lies at the vertex, where count = −b ÷ 2a. In practice, reading the lowest point directly from the chart is almost always sufficient.

### 7.4 Alternative method without a heart rate monitor

The test can be inverted by fixing effort rather than pace. Swim 50m repeats at a steady moderate effort, around 6 out of 10, at each stroke count, and record the time. The count that produces the fastest time for the same effort is the optimum. This method is less precise, because perceived effort drifts, but it becomes dependable when repeated.

### 7.5 Refinements

**The optimum shifts with speed.** The best count at easy pace will be higher than the best count at threshold pace, since faster swimming favors additional rate. Running the test at two or three paces yields an optimum for each, forming a line that guides training across intensities.

**Periodic retesting.** Repeat the test every six to eight weeks. As grip and power improve, the entire curve should shift toward lower counts at the same speed. That shift, rather than a lower count in isolation, is the evidence that technique is improving.

For the 170 cm swimmer in this review, an appropriate starting grid is 14 to 18 strokes per 25m at easy pace, with the optimum expected at 15 or 16, to be confirmed by the data.

## Chapter 8: Testing and Training Protocols

### 8.1 Establishing the baseline

Before progressive speed work, determine the baseline stroke count using the fixed-pace method in Section 7.3. The count at the bottom of the easy-pace curve serves as the starting count for the sets below.

### 8.2 The progressive speed set

This set identifies the slip threshold by increasing speed while attempting to hold stroke count.

- **Warm-up:** 400–600m, so early repeats are not slowed by stiffness.
- **Main set:** 8–12 × 50m, starting genuinely easy and taking 2–3 seconds off each repeat, progressing from easy toward near-maximum effort.
- **Starting count:** the baseline from Section 8.1 (likely 15–16), never an artificially low count achieved through glide.
- **Push-offs:** identical on every length, with a fixed breakout point such as the first stroke at about 5m, since push-off variation distorts counts.
- **Counting:** each 25m recorded separately; the second length usually breaks first.
- **Rest:** about 20–30 seconds early in the set, rising to 45–60 seconds for the fast repeats, so that fatigue does not break the count for the wrong reason.
- **Break criterion:** count rises by two or more on either length, or by one on two consecutive repeats. Stop at this point, or perform one confirming repeat.
- **Records:** time, count per 25m, and heart rate or perceived effort for each repeat.

Two rules protect the validity of the test. The count must not be preserved by gliding; if a pause is needed to hit the number, the count has effectively already broken. And the stroke should not be forced into a frantic, distorted shape to hold the number near the threshold; a slightly higher count with clean rhythm is preferable.

### 8.3 Ongoing training

Between tests, train with repeats at a pace just below the slip threshold while holding count. This is the zone in which adaptation occurs.

### 8.4 Supporting drills

Sculling develops the feel of pressure on the palm and forearm and the ability to find still water. Swimming a few lengths with closed fists, then with open hands, makes grip immediately apparent. Low-count lengths at 13 strokes can be used sparingly to reinforce body line and length.

### 8.5 Monitoring progress

Repeat the progressive speed set every three to four weeks. Improvement appears as the slip threshold moving to faster times at the same count. A useful supplementary measure is "swim golf," the sum of strokes and seconds for a 50m. The lowest score, not the lowest stroke count, marks the efficient zone.

## Chapter 9: Key Conclusions

1. Stroke count is a proxy for efficiency, not a measure of it. Efficiency is the energy cost per meter at a given speed.
2. Reducing count below a swimmer's optimum raises that cost through higher force per stroke, fast-twitch fiber recruitment, speed fluctuation, isometric tension and impaired breathing.
3. Practice makes a low-count stroke easier at slow speeds but cannot remove its physical cost at training and racing speeds.
4. Propulsion depends on grip rather than raw force. Moving large volumes of water gently is far more economical than moving small volumes forcefully.
5. Raising rate while holding count is the definition of getting faster; keeping the count increase small as speed rises is the practical target.
6. For a 170 cm swimmer, 17 strokes is a normal easy count, and the true optimum, with improved technique and no glide, is likely around 15–16.
7. A swimmer's personal optimum can be located by estimating a range from height, defining a glide floor and slip ceiling, and measuring heart rate across counts at a fixed pace; the bottom of the resulting curve marks the focus range.
8. Training should retain the technical gains of low-count work, discard the glide, and progressively raise the speed at which stroke count holds.
