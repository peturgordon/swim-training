01

What the evidence supports

Five search angles, 24 fetched sources, 106 candidate claims — each surviving claim below was put to three independent adversarial reviewers instructed to try to refute it. A claim needed two of three votes in its favor to survive. What's here is what didn't break.

Intensity distribution should be event-dependent, and polarized beats threshold-heavy at the elite level.

High confidence

A systematic review found sprint-event swimmers typically train polarized/threshold, middle-distance swimmers threshold/pyramidal, and long-distance swimmers pyramidal. In a controlled 6-week crossover on 22 elite juniors, a polarized split (81% easy / 4% moderate / 15% hard, by blood lactate) beat a threshold split (65/25/10) on 100m time-trial improvement — and produced less fatigue and better recovery. The practical read: the "comfortably-hard tempo" middle zone is the one to shrink, not the one to live in.

González-Ravé et al. 2021, systematic review ↗ Pla et al. 2019, RCT, n=22 ↗

Technique breaks down under fatigue through a measurable mechanism — this is real biomechanics, not just "getting tired."

High confidence

Intracycle velocity variation (the within-stroke speed wobble that signals a smooth vs. ragged pull) stays flat at easy effort but rises at maximal effort as swimmers fatigue and lose mechanical efficiency. Stroke count — a simpler proxy for the same thing — rises measurably as pace drops late in a fatiguing set. Holding form under load is a distinct, trainable coordination skill, not an automatic byproduct of aerobic fitness.

Fernandes et al. 2023, scoping review, 76 studies ↗ Cuenca-Fernández et al. 2021 ↗

Elite coaching is still dominated by "perfect technique" drilling in isolation — not by training technique to survive race conditions.

Medium confidence

A qualitative study of 20 elite coaches found part-task, drill-based methods remain the dominant approach, aimed at reinforcing one "correct" mechanical pattern — even though the same coaches report awareness of contemporary approaches built to make technique robust under varying, race-like conditions. This is self-reported coaching culture, not a controlled trial, but it names your exact complaint precisely: standard drilling isn't designed to transfer to speed or fatigue.

Brackley, Barris, Tor & Farrow 2020, n=20 coaches ↗

Practicing a skill while already fatigued measurably impairs learning it — separate from the fatigue just slowing you down in the moment.

High confidence

In a controlled experiment, a group that practiced a novel motor skill while physically fatigued learned it far slower within the same session than a fresh group (learning slope 0.038 vs. 0.169). A stronger claim — that this deficit lingers for days afterward — was tested and refuted, so treat the effect as session-local: drilling technique for the first time at the tail end of a hard set is a worse way to build it than drilling it fresh, then adding fatigue on top deliberately.

eLife 2019 ↗

Short race-pace repeats produce less acute fatigue than long ones at the same total volume — but the claim that they specifically protect technique did not survive verification.

Medium confidence

Ultra-short race-pace training (20×50m) produced significantly lower blood lactate than volume-matched traditional race-pace repeats (10×100m) at the same pace and work:rest ratio. That's confirmed. The more attractive follow-on claim — that the shorter format specifically preserves stroke mechanics better — was tested against three independent reviewers and refuted twice. Less fatigue per set is real; "therefore better technique" is not yet established.

Cuenca-Fernández et al. 2021 ↗ companion analysis, n=14 ↗

Elite open-water swimmers hold pace remarkably close to a lab-measured "critical speed" for the whole race — pacing off a real benchmark works.

Medium confidence

Top finishers in a 10km open-water race sustained roughly 92% of their pool-derived Critical Swimming Speed (CSS) from start to finish, with minimal decline — while lower-ranked swimmers faded. CSS itself is a simple, well-established field test (a 400m plus a 200m time trial), not a fad metric.

10km open-water pacing study ↗ USMS, CSS field test (secondary) ↗
02

What didn't hold up

Listed for transparency — these are the intuitively attractive answers that were explicitly tested and killed by adversarial review. None of the recommendations later in this document rely on them, even where they would have made a tidier story.

✗ 1–2

One dominant "wave-like" periodization model, used across elite swimming to build fitness and technique together — the elite literature itself doesn't cleanly settle this.

✗ 0–3

That ultra-short race-pace training (USRPT) lets swimmers bank more race-pace volume with better-preserved technique than longer repeats — the underlying stroke-count correlation didn't replicate under review.

✗ 1–2

That stroke length (not rate) is what separates elite 50m sprinters, and that gold-medal distance swimmers favor rate over length — both directions of this claim failed.

✗ 0–3

That elite swimmers hold a flatter velocity-variation curve than beginners as speed rises toward maximal effort.

✗ 0–3

That a coach-education program shifted cueing language from internal ("drive your elbow up") to external/holistic focus in a way shown to produce more robust skill execution.

✗ 0–3

That fatigue-related learning deficits persist for multiple days after the fatigue itself resolves.

03

Last season, audited

Reading all 65 logged Garpar sessions (Sept '25–May '26) end to end, one structural pattern repeats almost every time, and one clear inflection point splits the season in two.

The pace target tightened on a fixed schedule, independent of any measured baseline. Interval per 100m dropped from 2:30 to 2:00 in five steps over the season's first five months, then held flat for the remaining four. Explicit effort-zone language (z1 white through z5 purple, sometimes with target pulse) only starts appearing in the written sets from 1 Feb onward — by which point the pace standard had already reached its floor. The zone system arrived after the squeeze, not before it.

The recurring shape of a session

Nearly every one of the 65 sessions follows the same skeleton: Upphitun (warmup, often with fins) → Tækni (technique, almost always fins and/or paddles, at reduced speed, isolating one cue — high elbow, catch-up, single-arm, sculling) → Aðalsett (main set, usually pull-buoy-and-paddles tempo work plus a shorter maximal-effort portion) → a short cooldown. It's a well-built skeleton, and the drill vocabulary is genuinely broad — sculling, catch-up, fist swimming, high-elbow ("gilligill"), single-arm, kick isolation, all four strokes rotate through it regularly.

What doesn't happen, in any of the 65 sessions, is a step between the drill and the maximal effort that keeps the day's technique cue active while speed rises. Technique is taught slow-and-isolated; speed is trained separately and described only by effort tag ("z5", "útopnu", "á fullu"). The two never share a set.

Max-effort sets favor short reps — but recovery is often too short to protect them

The genuinely maximal portions are usually short (15–25m within a 50), which is the right instinct — short reps at true top speed are how neural/speed adaptations are trained without accumulated fatigue wrecking form. But rest is frequently 10–30s for these reps (e.g. 8×50, 25m all-out / 25m easy, 10s rest), which is short enough that later reps in the set are executed increasingly fatigued — closer to lactate-tolerance conditioning than to protected, high-quality speed work. A handful of sets (30–60s rest on z4/z5 portions) get this right already.

400m is trained as continuous volume, not as a fatigue-management problem

Distances up to 500–600m appear regularly, and 200–500m repeats at z2/z3 are common — real aerobic volume is there. But nowhere in the season is a ~400m effort deliberately broken down and paired with an explicit technique-holding cue the way the log already does well for pull-buoy sets (a few sessions ask swimmers to count strokes per 50 and "hold the same number" through a pull-buoy set — genuinely good technique-under-load practice that just never gets applied to a straight, un-aided 400m.)

No visible testing, blocks, or taper

The only individualized numbers in the whole season are the fixed clock intervals (@2:00, @1:10, etc.) and the load score Commit assigns; there's no recorded time trial, CSS test, or personal-benchmark session anywhere in the log, and no visible base/build/peak structure — training runs as one continuous stream from September to May. Starts and turns get brief, late-season practice time (March onward), suggesting a spring meet was on the calendar, but nothing in the log shows a deliberate taper toward it.

A framework for your three sessions

Six principles, each tagged with how firmly it's grounded — then a concrete weekly shape you can run with your existing stroke mix (four strokes, tilted toward crawl, same as last season).

01

Widen the split; thin the middle

Within each session, aim most non-technique swimming clearly easy (conversational, z1–z2) or clearly hard (near-max, z4–z5). Deliberately shrink "good tempo" pull-buoy volume rather than defaulting to it as the main set.

Grounded in: polarized-vs-threshold RCT, high confidence
02

Add the missing middle step: a bridge set

Keep drilling fresh and slow as you already do. Then, before the hardest effort, insert a short set that carries the day's cue into rising speed — e.g. 6×50 building 25→50 while holding the cue — instead of jumping straight from slow drill to all-out effort.

Grounded in: fatigue-impairs-learning study + coaching-practice gap, high/medium confidence
03

Protect the quality of maximal reps with real recovery

Keep sprint reps short (15–25m, as last season already favored) but lengthen rest on the truly maximal portions specifically — 60–90s+ rather than 10–20s — so each rep is swum near top speed with form intact. Save short-rest, high-volume reps for separate conditioning work, not technique work.

Extrapolated from the fatigue-mechanism evidence — the "USRPT protects technique" claim itself was refuted, so treat this as informed design, not a proven protocol
04

Train the exact failure point: a tracked 400m

Once every week or two, swim a straight or lightly-broken ~400m while counting strokes per length and trying to hold the count as fatigue sets in — the same "count and hold" idea last season already used well for pull-buoy sets, just applied without aids to the distance that actually gives you trouble.

Direct application of the stroke-count/IVV fatigue findings, medium confidence
05

Get a real number to train against

Test Critical Swim Speed periodically — a 400m time trial plus a 200m time trial, roughly every 6–8 weeks — and set your own interval targets off that instead of the squad's shared clock. Useful both for tracking "am I getting faster" and for pacing endurance sets honestly.

Grounded in: CSS field-test literature + open-water pacing study, medium confidence
06

Plan in blocks, not one long straight line

Rather than one interval tightening steadily for eight months, run roughly 4–6 week blocks with a shifting emphasis — an aerobic-base block, a block leaning into the bridge-set and top-end-speed work above, a pre-meet block adding race-simulation and a short taper.

No direct masters-specific evidence exists for this — flagged as the one recommendation resting on general principle, not a study

Three session types, one week

Day A — Base & Technique

mostly z1–z2
  1. Pre-pool prehab · 5minBanded Y-T-W retractions + thoracic rotations — wakes up the muscles that hold a high-elbow catch together, before the day's technique focus.
  2. Warmup · 10–12minEasy swim, mixed strokes, one loosening drill.
  3. Technique · 15minSlow, fins-assisted, one cue only (e.g. high-elbow catch). Crawl-heavy, one non-crawl stroke rotated in.
  4. Bridge · 8min4–6×50 building 25→50 holding today's cue, easy pace ceiling.
  5. Aerobic main · 15–18min4–6×200 or continuous 800–1000 at genuinely easy z1–z2 — CSS pace or below once tested.
  6. Cooldown · 5minVery easy, any stroke.

Day B — Speed Bridge

z1 + z4–z5
  1. Pre-pool prehab · 5minBanded external rotations + push-up plus — primes the rotator cuff and serratus anterior before the week's highest-load reps.
  2. Warmup · 10minEasy swim + 4×25 build.
  3. Technique · 12minFins/paddles drill on a crawl catch or rotation cue.
  4. Bridge · 10min6–8×50 building to near-max over the last 15–25m, cue still active, full recovery (30–40s).
  5. Max effort · 12–15min6–10×15–25m truly all-out, 60–90s recovery — quality over volume, stop the set if form visibly breaks.
  6. Cooldown · 6minEasy, technique-focused.

Day C — Race Distance

z2–z3 + fatigue focus
  1. Pre-pool prehab · 5minSide planks + dynamic hip/ankle mobility — supports body position and kick mechanics through the longest set of the week.
  2. Warmup · 10minEasy swim, other three strokes rotated in.
  3. Technique · 10minShort, one cue, moderate pace (not slow) — priming for the main set.
  4. Race-distance set · 20–25minEvery 1–2 weeks: ~400m broken into 100s or 200s, counting strokes/length, holding the count as pace/fatigue rises. Other weeks: 4–6×100–200 @ CSS pace, holding form as the primary metric.
  5. Cooldown · 5minEasy, any stroke.
This keeps the four-stroke rotation and the crawl tilt from last season — technique and warmup blocks are the natural place to rotate back/breast/fly through, while the bridge, max-effort, and race-distance work stay crawl-focused, matching both your goals and the group's existing emphasis.
On the prehab additions: the exercise categories (thoracic mobility, rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer activation, core anti-rotation) are standard, sensible sports-medicine practice — but no research in this corpus validates a specific dose for this population. The best available injury review found the evidence too weak to set a validated cutoff for any age group. Treat 5 minutes of this as prudent general conditioning, not a proven protocol.

Lane management for a shared group

Everything above assumes you can actually swim your own pace in a shared lane. A masters lane usually mixes abilities — a few practical, non-evidence-graded habits keep that from undermining the framework.

PracticeWhat it meansWhy
CSS-based lane assignmentGroup by tested CSS pace, not reputation or historyKeeps everyone in a lane actually training in the same zone
Front / Middle / Back rolesFront sets pace and leaves on the interval; Middle drafts and rotates into the lead on drill sets; Back adjusts for an off-day, a stroke change, or tight shouldersGives every lane position a legitimate job, not just "keeping up"
Staggered send-offsLeave ~5s apart, especially on faster repsKeeps clear water at the walls, avoids congestion and swum-over turns
Fins/buoy as equalizerWhoever can't hold the lane's send-off interval that day uses fins or a pull buoy rather than skipping repsKeeps the group's rep count and rhythm intact without forcing an uneven effort comparison
This is practitioner coaching-logistics knowledge, not something put through adversarial verification like the rest of this document — useful group-management practice regardless of evidence tier.

Tracking progress without a meet

No race on the calendar means no finish-line clock to check against. These three buckets replace it — each tells you something different, and none require more than a watch and a notebook.

Pace

are you getting faster
  1. CSS test400m + 200m time trial, every 6–8 weeks. Your cleanest single trend line — and it recalibrates every interval in the framework above off your own pace instead of a placeholder.
  2. Repeated benchmark setSame distance, same target effort, run periodically (e.g. monthly) — track the average time trending down.

Efficiency

is technique holding up
  1. Stroke count per 25mAt a fixed pace, tracked over weeks. Falling count at the same pace means a more efficient pull.
  2. Stroke count held under fatigueThe delta between early-set and late-set count during a hard or long effort. This is the most evidence-grounded number here — it's the exact proxy the verified research uses for technique breaking down under fatigue, and it speaks directly to your stated problem.
  3. SWOLFStroke count + seconds per length, the number most swim watches surface. A useful smoothed trend, but a coaching heuristic, not something in the verified research — layer it on top of stroke count, don't treat it as an independent signal.
  4. Stroke rateStrokes/min, via tempo trainer or watch. Paired with distance-per-stroke it shows how you're getting faster. Which one to prioritize was itself one of the refuted claims in the research, so track both rather than optimizing toward either.

Effort

is the engine improving
  1. Heart rate at fixed paceLower HR for the same pace over months signals real aerobic improvement. A supplementary research pass confirmed this isn't just borrowed from running/cycling — a direct swimming-specific study found the same heart-rate "drift" mechanism during sustained effort, tracking rising stress hormones rather than dehydration. What isn't validated: any specific numeric "decoupling %" threshold — treat those as a coaching-platform heuristic, not a proven cutoff. Wrist-optical HR is unreliable mid-stroke (water pressure + arm motion); a swim-rated chest strap works, or the manual 10s pulse count the Garpar log already uses ("púls ~120") — just log it consistently.
  2. RPE (1–10)No equipment needed. The same pace feeling easier over time is a meaningful signal on its own.
  3. Session loadRPE × duration, summed weekly — a quick check that training stress is actually progressing rather than repeating.

08

Open questions

What would sharpen this from a framework into an actual dated program.

Meet calendar: the recommended block structure (principle 06) can't be scheduled without knowing when — or whether — a masters meet is on your calendar this season. If there's a date, the pre-meet block and taper should count backward from it.
Baseline numbers: a first CSS test (400m + 200m time trial) would let every interval in this framework be set to your actual pace rather than a placeholder — worth doing in the first week of whatever comes next.
Which specific drills carry over: no confirmed research directly ranks catch-up vs. sculling vs. fist-swim vs. high-elbow drills by how well they transfer to race pace — the coaching-practice finding above speaks to the drilling paradigm being under-bridged, not to any one drill being wrong. Worth treating your existing drill list as fine and focusing the change on principles 02–04 above.
