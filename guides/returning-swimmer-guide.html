The Returning Swimmer

You already know how to swim. What's missing is the engine. A simple mental model for structuring each session, each week, and rebuilding a real aerobic base — for someone who trained seriously once, took years off, and is back in the water now.

00

Read this first

In your teenage years your body already built efficient stroke mechanics and a strong aerobic engine — the cardiovascular and metabolic conditioning that lets you hold a hard pace for a long time without your heart rate climbing to its ceiling. Getting back in the water doesn't start you from zero: a genuine training history leaves a real physiological floor above a true beginner's, on both counts. What isn't settled is whether stroke feel or cardiovascular fitness comes back faster — treat them as two separate rebuilds on their own timelines, each taking months of patient, specific work, rather than assuming one arrives first.

The single most common mistake for a returning competitive swimmer is training at the intensity their technique still allows, rather than the intensity their current engine can sustain. That gap is where plateaus, staleness, and shoulder injuries live. This guide is built around closing it deliberately.

01

The three engines

Every set you swim runs on one of three engines. Knowing which one you're running — and how much time to spend on each — is the single most useful idea in this guide.

Steady state, plainly: it's what happens when a swim stays entirely inside the aerobic engine even as the distance gets long — heart rate holds flat instead of climbing toward its ceiling. Everything in this guide points toward widening that engine.
02

Structure within one session

No matter which engine is the day's focus, every session follows the same shape.

One session, one primary job. Trying to build aerobic base and chase top speed and grind technique in the same hour is how you end up merely tired in all three, and good at none. Let the main work section commit to a single engine.
03

Structure across a week

With three sessions a week, spread the three jobs across them rather than mixing all three into every session.

Session 1

aerobic engine

Easy volume plus technique work. This is where the steady-state base actually gets built — most of the session sits in the wide left band of the engine diagram above.

Session 2

sprint engine

Short, genuinely maximal efforts with real recovery between them (60–90s+), bridged in gradually from a technique cue at rising speed — not a flat-out swim from a cold start.

Session 3

steady-state distance

The longest continuous or lightly-broken swim of the week, at a pace deliberately chosen to keep heart rate flat — this is the session that directly trains and tests the steady-state goal.

Add the three sessions up over a full week and roughly 80% of your total swimming time should be easy (Session 1's aerobic work plus the easy portions of the other two), with a smaller slice genuinely hard, and as little as possible spent in the grey zone. That "80/20" split is a well-known simplification used across endurance sports — the underlying swim-specific trial found a similar mix (mostly easy, a touch of moderate, a meaningful chunk hard) outperformed a moderate-heavy week on both improvement and fatigue.

04

Structure across a season

Training in one unbroken straight line for months either flattens out or breaks something. Think in waves instead: a stretch of mostly widening the aerobic base, then a stretch that leans harder into speed and steady-state distance on top of that base, then back to base-building before it starts to fray. With no meet on the calendar to count backward from, just let each wave run roughly 4–6 weeks before shifting emphasis — and don't be afraid to extend a base-building wave if progress is still coming; there's no clock forcing you to rush it.

05

The steady-state goal, explained

If you swim at a pace beyond what your current aerobic engine can sustain, your heart rate creeps upward over the swim even though the pace and effort feel constant — eventually it hits its ceiling and forces you to slow down or stop. This drift is sometimes called decoupling: pace stays flat, heart rate doesn't. The mechanism itself is well-confirmed physiology, including a direct swimming-specific study — but the exact numeric "decoupling %" thresholds some coaching apps report are a practitioner heuristic, not something research has validated. Use the flat-vs-climbing comparison below, not a specific percentage.

A simple protocol to build and test it

1

Pick a pace that feels genuinely easy — conversational, form intact, no strain.

2

Swim continuously at that pace. Check your heart rate (a 10-second pulse count, or a swim-rated strap) roughly a third of the way through, and again near the end.

3

Close readings → you're in steady state at that pace and distance. Next time, gently extend the distance, or nudge the pace.

4

A noticeably higher late reading → that combination is currently above your ceiling. Keep the distance, back off the pace, and retest later.

5

Repeat over months. The pace/distance where the line stays flat should keep expanding — that expansion is the goal, made measurable.

This is also, almost exactly, general cardiovascular health training. Widening the aerobic base doesn't trade off against the "general health" goal — it's the same work, so all four goals in this guide point in the same direction.
06

Do this. Avoid this.

Do

  • Spend most pool minutes at a genuinely easy, conversational effort — most sessions, most of each session.
  • Track something — a fixed-pace pulse check, a stroke count, a benchmark swim — so you can tell "harder" from "better."
  • Do technique work first, every session, while fresh.
  • Give real sprint efforts real recovery — 60–90s or more — so each one stays high quality.
  • Increase volume or pace in small steps, over months, not weeks.
  • Warm up fully before anything hard.
  • Let each session commit to one primary job.

Avoid

  • Swimming most sets at the "comfortably hard" grey-zone effort by default.
  • Chasing the pace you held twenty years ago — your fitness and your technique rebuild on their own separate timelines, and it's not settled which comes back faster.
  • Drilling technique at the end of a tired, hard set.
  • Judging a session only by how tired you feel — grey-zone effort feels hard and teaches little; aerobic-base effort feels easy and is exactly right.
  • Ignoring a heart rate that keeps climbing during a "steady" swim — that's information, not weakness.
  • Stacking hard days back to back, or skipping rest days.
07

A note on coming back after years away

Your shoulders are the first bill that comes due. A returning swimmer's stroke often lets them do more volume than their tendons and rotator cuffs are currently conditioned for — the classic overuse pattern in this sport. The research on swim shoulder injuries is real but, by its own authors' admission, too weak to pin down an exact safe volume cutoff for any age group — so treat "build volume gradually, back off at the first twinge" as prudent caution, not a precise rule with a validated number behind it. A few minutes of dryland work before you get in the water is worth adding on general principle, even without a validated dose: thoracic mobility (open-book stretches, foam rolling) to keep the upper back rotating instead of the shoulder overcompensating, rotator-cuff and scapular-stabilizer activation (banded external rotations, Y-T-W raises) since these are the muscles that fatigue and let the joint drift out of position, and core anti-rotation work (side planks) so the trunk, not the shoulder, absorbs stroke torque.

Ignore the ghost of your younger self. Comparing today's pace to a personal best from decades ago is demoralizing and not useful — the only comparison that matters is your own trend over the coming months.

Patience compounds — and you're not starting from zero. Detraining research on previously well-trained athletes is genuinely encouraging here: fitness built over a real training history doesn't fully disappear even after years off. Both cardiovascular and muscular adaptations decline but plateau above where a true beginner starts — you're rebuilding from a real foundation, not from scratch. Whether stroke feel or cardiovascular fitness comes back faster isn't something research has directly compared, so give both the time they need rather than assuming one arrives first.

Quick glossary

Aerobic engine
Effort your body can fuel with oxygen alone — sustainable for a long time.
Grey zone / threshold
Comfortably-hard effort at the edge of sustainable. Useful in small doses; overused by instinct.
Sprint / anaerobic
Effort beyond what oxygen alone can fuel — only sustainable for seconds to a couple of minutes.
Steady state
A pace and distance where heart rate holds flat rather than drifting upward.
Decoupling / drift
When heart rate climbs at a constant pace — a sign the aerobic engine isn't yet built for that pace.
CSS
Critical Swim Speed — a simple pace benchmark from a 400m plus a 200m time trial.
RPE
Rate of perceived exertion — how hard something felt, on a scale of 1–10.
